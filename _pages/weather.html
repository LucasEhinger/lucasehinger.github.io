Quick Overview
This site uses atmospheric model data from NOAA's HRRR, RAP, NAM, and GFS models, the ECMWF open data, and the National Blend of Models (NBM) to generate forecasts for undercast probability at several summits in New Hampshire. Forecast resolution, accuracy, and update frequency vary between the models.
Model Forecast Range (Hours) Spatial Resolution Strengths Weaknesses
HRRR 0-48 3 km High temporal resolution, good for short-term forecasts Limited range, may struggle with longer-term trends
NAM 0-60 3 km Good for medium-range forecasts, covers larger areas Lower resolution compared to HRRR, less accurate for local events
GFS 0-120 13 km Long-range forecasts, global coverage Coarser resolution, may miss localized weather phenomena
RAP 0-48 13 km Hourly regional refresh (HRRR's parent model) Coarser than HRRR, short range only
ECMWF 0-48 ~0.25° (~28 km) Highly skilled global dynamics and thermodynamics No cloud fields in open data; 3-hourly steps
NBM 0-48 ~2.5 km Calibrated blend; direct ceiling & visibility, incl. probabilistic Surface fields only (no upper-air); blends can smooth extremes
The forecasts you see on NWS and other sites are often based on a combination of these models along with observational data and expert analysis. Trust them, before you trust my plots, which come from individual models and are interpreted by a dumb particle physicist.

I built this to visualize data not readily available from the NWS website. While exact wind speeds from an individual, non-local, coarse-grain model may be inaccurate, larger trends such as sky coverage and cloud elevation can be informative. Full model details can be viewed here.

Bar height is the model’s score for that hour as a share of its threshold — the score at or above which it calls an undercast. The dashed line is that threshold. Each forecast lead gets its own, fitted on a held-out year to give the best balance of false alarms against misses, and it falls with distance because the model grows less confident further out.
