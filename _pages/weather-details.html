As an avid hiker, undercast conditions (where the clouds are below you) are quite a treat. They’re also often unexpected. While the surprise can be fun, I also enjoy knowing whether I can expect nice views, or should go tree skiing instead. As such, I set out to predict undercast conditions in New Hampshire’s White Mountains.

I began this project assuming I could grab cloud base height information from existing weather reports (such as Windy), and use that to forecast undercast conditions. When that failed to have the desired predictive power, I queried weather forecast data from a variety of sources, specifically looking for inversions. I quickly discovered that undercast conditions are far more complex than a simple temperature inversion; or at minimum, global models have a hard time predicting local inversions. As a result, I arrived at the models described below: an ensemble of three machine-learning algorithms, trained on forecasts from six weather models (HRRR, RAP, NAM, GFS, ECMWF, and NBM) against a record of when it was actually undercast. Building that record turned out to be most of the work, and is described next.

About Undercasts

While academic literature about undercast skies is sparse (it’s almost like climate scientists have something more important to study), there is a general consensus on what conditions are needed to produce undercast skies.

First and foremost, a temperature inversion is required, where temperature increases with height (rather than decreasing), trapping a cold layer of air at lower temperatures.

Determining if it’s undercast

To validate any model (ML or otherwise), we need to know if it was actually undercast. Hiking up every day is the ideal way to find out; I also need to finish my PhD at some point. What follows is the compromise — three steps that turn one year of hand-labeled webcam images into a 29-year record.

Step 1 — Label a year of webcam footage by hand

The Mt. Washington Observatory publishes timelapse video from two cameras: one on the tower and one looking out from the observatory itself. Both hold roughly one usable frame per day around local noon. I pulled the frames, OCR’d the burned-in timestamps to date them, and then sat down and scored every single one by eye — tower and observatory views judged separately, then averaged, with anything at or above 0.5 counted as undercast.

That gives 589 labeled days spanning 25 May 2024 to 3 January 2026, of which 24 are undercast. It was manual, and it was not fun.

It is also, on its own, nowhere near enough. Twenty-four positive examples cannot train a classifier on a hundred-odd weather parameters. So the hand labels are not used as the training set — they are used as the answer key for working out what an undercast looks like in a data source that goes back decades.

Step 2 — Work out what the summit observer writes down when it’s undercast

Mount Washington is one of the few remaining staffed weather stations in the country. Every hour, a human being on the summit looks out the window and augments the station’s METAR report with plain-text remarks. The group that matters here is TPS LWR — “tops lower” — which gives the coverage and the top height of any cloud deck sitting below the 6,288 ft summit. Nobody else reports this, because almost nowhere else is there a person standing above the clouds to report it.

A real report, from a day I scored undercast:

KMWN 211559Z 06010KT 70SM SCT/// OVC070 09/M06 RMK TPS LWR SCT040 SUN DMLY VSBL BINOVC
KMWN Station. Mount Washington summit, 6,288 ft.
211559Z Filed on the 21st at 15:59 UTC — just before local noon.
06010KT Wind from 060° at 10 knots. A calm day up there.
70SM Visibility 70 statute miles. Eyeballed off distant landmarks, because someone is standing there. Automated stations stop at 10.
SCT/// Scattered layer, height unknown — the /// means the observer could not assign one. Note this is not evidence the summit is in cloud.
OVC070 Overcast at 7,000 ft above the station — so ~13,300 ft above sea level. This is the lid overhead, and at 7,000 ft it is high enough to leave the view intact.
09/M06 Temperature 9 °C, dewpoint −6 °C. The gap means the air at the summit is nowhere near saturated.
RMK Everything after this is the observer’s free text.
TPS LWR SCT040 The group this whole project rests on. “Tops lower”: a scattered deck whose tops reach 4,000 ft above sea level — 2,288 ft below the observer.
SUN DMLY VSBL Sun dimly visible through the layer above.
BINOVC “Breaks in overcast” — gaps in the lid.

The datum flips halfway through. Heights in the body of a METAR (OVC070) are measured above the station; heights after TPS LWR are above sea level. Same report, two different zero points — get that backwards and a deck 2,288 ft below you reads as a layer 4,000 ft above you.

Seeing it once makes it click: the observation viewer puts 42 of these reports next to the webcam frames taken minutes earlier, so you can look at the photographs and the observer’s words together — including the days where my scoring and the automated screen disagree.

So: take the 589 hand-labeled days, pull the observer’s report nearest noon on each one, and ask which combination of conditions best separates the 24 undercast days from the other 565. That search produced five cuts:

Condition Why
TPS LWR coverage is broken or overcast At least 5/8 of the sky below you. A scattered deck is a broken-up deck — including it cost more in false alarms than the extra catches were worth.
visibility > 40 SM You have to be able to see the deck. Because the station is staffed, visibility is eyeballed off distant landmarks and routinely reported out to 130 SM — automated stations cap out at 10.
no ceiling overhead, or one at or above 5,000 ft A low lid overhead makes it a sandwich, not an undercast.
deck top at least 500 ft below the summit A deck 200 ft down is not a sea of clouds, it’s weather happening at your feet.
lowest layer of any coverage overhead at or above 1,000 ft The ceiling test only looks at broken and overcast layers, so it happily passes a report carrying FEW002 — cloud touching the summit. You cannot look down on a deck from inside the muck.

That last cut was the single best addition: it throws out nine of sixteen false alarms and costs not one real undercast day. It is also stable — anywhere between 500 and 1,000 ft scores the same — which is reassuring, because a threshold that only works at one exact value is usually a threshold fitted to noise.

Scored against the full 589 hand-labeled days, the finished screen gets precision 0.70 and recall 0.67: when it says undercast it’s right about seven times in ten, and it catches about two thirds of the real ones.

Step 3 — Run the screen over 29 years

The observer remarks are archived. Once the screen is fixed, it can be pointed at the entire back catalogue: 253,317 hourly observations from 1997 through 2026, of which 6,852 pass as undercast. That is roughly 285 times more positive examples than hand-labeling produced, from one year of squinting at webcam stills.

1997 is not an arbitrary start. The remark format changes underneath you as you go back: before 1997 the observers used a spaced variant that frequently omits the height altogether (TPS LWR SCT NE-SW), and the deck-depth cut needs that height. 1997 is the first year the canonical TPS LWR SCT035 form is used consistently, so that is where the record starts.

Each observation is labeled on its own, not rolled up into a daily verdict. A deck that exists at 6 am and has burned off by 2 pm is two different atmospheric states, and the forecast parameters are sampled at each observation’s own valid time to match.

One wrinkle: the reported rate drifts upward (click to expand)

The fraction of observations passing the screen climbs steadily through the record — about 1.2% in 1997 against 5.4% in 2024. Undercast conditions on Mount Washington did not quadruple in thirty years. What changed is the reporting: TPS LWR went from an occasional note to something observers record routinely, so the later years are simply more thoroughly documented.

The rise splits into two factors, and both are about writing rather than weather: observers mention a deck below the summit 2.6× more often than in 1997, and those mentions are 1.7× more likely to be complete enough to clear the screen (2.6 × 1.7 gives the 4.5× rise in the top panel). Note also that this is a noisy drift, not a steady climb — 2025 and 2026 fall back toward the long-run level.

Two consequences. First, this record cannot be read as a climate trend — it is a record of what was written down, and the writing habits moved. Second, for training it is an outright trap: a model handed the date alongside the weather could score respectably by learning “recent years have more undercast” while learning nothing about the atmosphere. The fix is in how the comparison cases are chosen — every undercast observation is matched against non-undercast observations drawn from the same year, same month, and same hour of day, so the calendar and the clock carry no signal and the weather parameters have to do the work.

What this record is not (click to expand)

Every field above comes out of a human being’s written observation — the remark text and the hand-coded METAR body. None of it is an instrument reading. That is the whole reason the dataset exists (no sensor reports “there is a sea of cloud 2,000 ft below me”), and also its central limitation.

So the 6,852 undercast observations are a proxy, not truth. At precision 0.70, roughly three in ten are something other than a clean undercast. And recall is capped near 0.79 by the remarks themselves: of the eight labeled undercast days the screen misses, five report a scattered deck (deliberately excluded) and two report the summit fogged in at 1/16 SM — the observer could not see the deck that the webcam could.

Because of that, the hand-labeled days are never used for training. They are held back as an independent test set, so that a model trained on the screen’s labels can be scored against human judgement of actual photographs rather than against the screen that produced its training data.

Status: the 29-year record is built, the forecast parameters have been downloaded at every observation’s own valid time for three leads, and the models are retrained — the headline numbers are in Results. The confusion matrices, feature-importance charts and per-source tables further down have not been regenerated and still describe the first pass, which had only the 24 hand-labeled undercast days to learn from and sampled its forecast fields at the wrong time of day.

What that buys

Hand-labeling a year of webcam frames produced 589 days carrying 24 undercast examples. Reading the observer’s own words over the same criteria produced 253,317 hourly observations carrying 6,852 — roughly 286× more positive examples, and enough that a classifier can see more than a handful of cases. From that pool a stratified sample of 24,545 observations is drawn for training, which is where the next section starts.

First Attempt

Scientific consensus is that undercast conditions are caused by temperature inversions. I figured it would be easy to query a couple of NOAA models, look for when temperature inversions were predicted, and voila, I have an undercast predictor. No machine learning required.

Turns out, predicting localized weather patterns in the mountains is hard — and more so on the most prominent peak for almost 1,000 miles, one renowned for its harsh weather. But “hard” is not a number, so here is the actual experiment. Four hand-written rules, each a statement about the atmosphere rather than a fitted model, with their thresholds tuned on the training split only and then applied untouched to the same holdouts the classifiers are scored on.

Rule In words
A inversion Temperature at 850 mb warmer than at 925 mb. That layer sits just below the summit, so a positive difference is a capping inversion exactly where an undercast’s lid should be.
B summit above the deck The model reports no cloud ceiling, or one above 1,917 m — i.e. it does not think the summit itself is in the murk.
C = A and B An inversion, with the summit out of it.
D = C and sees far Plus modelled surface visibility above a threshold — you have to be able to see the deck for it to count.

How they do

Scored on the base-rate holdout — a full unsampled year, where undercast really is about 3% of hours, so precision reads honestly:

Source Best rule Precision Recall False alarms
HRRR D 0.09 0.60 1,651
NAM D 0.07 0.53 1,879
RAP D 0.07 0.48 1,743
ECMWF A (no ceiling field published) 0.06 0.68 1,975

So the physics-only approach works, in the narrow sense that it beats guessing: at a 3% base rate, precision 0.09 is a threefold lift, and it catches 60% of real undercasts. It is also unusable. Acting on HRRR’s best rule means 1,651 false alarms in a single year — about four and a half wrong calls every day, to catch 154 real events.

Two rules that seemed obvious and were wrong (click to expand)

“Modelled cloud top below the summit.” This is the most natural way to say “the deck is beneath me,” and it is backwards. Rows where HRRR puts the cloud top below 1,917 m have a 9.2% undercast rate against a 16.8% base rate in that sample, while rows where HRRR reports no cloud at all run at 21.5%. The reason is resolution: HRRR’s 3 km terrain flattens this grid cell to roughly 1,200 m, some 700 m below the real summit. The model has no atmosphere between its own ground and the place the observer is standing, so it cannot represent “the deck tops out below me” — it can only say whether its cell is in cloud. Which is why rule B ended up phrased as the absence of a ceiling.

“And low cloud cover is high.” Adding a low-cloud-fraction term makes every combination worse — F1 on the training split drops from 0.41 to 0.09. Same cause: at this grid cell the low-cloud field describes cloud around the model’s own ground, which is not the deck below the peak.

Both failures point the same way. The pressure-level temperatures are trustworthy here because a pressure surface does not care what the model thinks the terrain is doing; anything keyed to height above the model’s ground is measuring a mountain 700 m shorter than the real one.

For what it is worth, the machine-learning models below roughly double that precision from the same HRRR fields, and the combined model does about five times better — which is the honest argument for bothering with any of it. The rules are not a strawman: they are re-derived, tuned and scored by scripts/baseline_undercast_rules.py on every run, so the comparison stays current.

The Model

You can play around with a couple of different examples of undercast, clear, and cloudy days in the region below (link), and no obvious relation jumps out by eye. For a long time I took that further than the evidence warranted and wrote here that the models simply do not predict the inversions — that they were too shallow to show up. Measuring it properly over 29 years says otherwise: the 850 mb–925 mb temperature difference, which is exactly the capping inversion just below the summit, is on its own the single most informative number in the entire dataset. The signal is there. It is just weak enough, and tangled enough with everything else, that no single threshold gets at it — which is what the rule-based attempt above demonstrates and what makes this a reasonable machine learning problem rather than an arithmetic one.

Key Description
cloud_top Cloud top height (AGL). The altitude of the highest extent of a cloud layer, useful for identifying deep convection or storm tops.
boundary_layer_cloud_layer Boundary layer cloud cover (%). Total cloud fraction within the planetary boundary layer, the lowest ~1–2 km of the atmosphere.
low_cloud_layer_percent Low cloud cover (%). Fractional cloud coverage below ~2 km (~6,500 ft), typically stratus or stratocumulus.
middle_cloud_layer_percent Mid-level cloud cover (%). Fractional cloud coverage between ~2–6 km (~6,500–20,000 ft), typically altocumulus or altostratus.
high_cloud_layer_percent High cloud cover (%). Fractional cloud coverage above ~6 km (~20,000 ft), typically cirrus or cirrostratus.
cloud_ceiling_m Cloud ceiling height (meters AGL). The height of the lowest broken or overcast cloud layer, critical for aviation and visibility assessment.
cloud_base_m Cloud base height (meters AGL). The altitude of the bottom of the lowest cloud layer, closely related to the Lifted Condensation Level (LCL).
cloud_top_pres Cloud top pressure (Pa). The atmospheric pressure at the top of the cloud layer — lower pressure = higher altitude cloud tops.
cloud_base_pres Cloud base pressure (Pa). The atmospheric pressure at the base of the cloud layer, an alternative vertical coordinate to height.
cloud_top_hgt Cloud top geopotential height (meters). Similar to cloud_top but expressed as geopotential height rather than AGL, more consistent across terrain.
wind_10m_day_max Daily maximum 10m wind speed (m/s). The peak wind speed at 10 meters above ground over the forecast day, useful for fire weather and wind advisories.
tmp_500mb Temperature at 500 mb (~5,500 m). Mid-troposphere temperature, used to assess atmospheric instability and identify cold upper-level troughs.
tmp_700mb Temperature at 700 mb (~3,000 m). Lower-mid troposphere temperature, important for identifying warm/cold advection and orographic effects.
tmp_850mb Temperature at 850 mb (~1,500 m). Low-level temperature commonly used as a proxy for surface airmass character and snow level estimation.
tmp_925mb Temperature at 925 mb (~750 m). Near-surface temperature just above the boundary layer, useful for detecting inversions and cold air damming.
tmp_1000mb Temperature at 1000 mb (~100 m). Near sea-level temperature, representing conditions very close to the surface in low-elevation areas.
tmp_2m 2-meter air temperature (°C). Standard screen-level temperature used in most surface weather forecasts and human comfort assessments.
rh_2m 2-meter relative humidity (%). Moisture content of the air at screen level, directly relevant to fire weather, fog formation, and human comfort.
hpbl_surface Planetary boundary layer height (meters). The depth of the turbulent mixing layer above the surface — higher in the afternoon, lower at night and during inversions.
hgt_0C_iso Height of the 0°C isotherm (meters). The freezing level altitude, critical for determining rain/snow transitions and icing conditions.
vis_surface Surface visibility (meters). Forecast horizontal visibility at the surface, reduced by fog, precipitation, smoke, or dust.
prate_surface Instantaneous precipitation rate (kg/m²/s). The rate of precipitation at a given forecast hour, useful for identifying intensity of ongoing precip.
apcp_surface Accumulated total precipitation (kg/m²). Cumulative precipitation over the forecast period, equivalent to mm of liquid-equivalent rainfall.

Model selection & training

We don't have a lot of data to train on, so I only consider relatively simple ML models. To begin, I trained four different classification models—Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost, and Gradient Boosting. Logistic Regression performed significantly worse, so the other three are kept and studied further. This trio is trained independently for each of the seven models above.

Undercast days are rare, so the classes are heavily imbalanced. Rather than synthesizing samples, each model handles this with class weights (Random Forest's balanced class weights, XGBoost's scale_pos_weight, and balanced sample weights for Gradient Boosting), which suits tree ensembles and keeps the pipeline clean.

Because each date contributes many forecast-hour rows, every cross-validation fold is grouped by date, so no single day's rows land in both training and scoring — otherwise the model would "see the answer" for a day it is being tested on. (An earlier version split rows randomly and looked far more accurate than it really was.) Performance is measured with five-fold grouped cross-validation, and each model's decision threshold is chosen on the pooled out-of-fold predictions rather than a single split: undercast is so rare that any one validation fold holds only a day or two of it. The threshold targets the most recall it can while holding precision above a floor, so the deployed model leans toward staying quiet unless the signal is reasonably strong.

How well does it work? The three algorithms all rank undercast days well — ROC-AUC sits around 0.80–0.87 across the seven data sources (NBM and the combined all-parameters model are strongest). Precision is the hard part: because undercast is only about 4% of days, even good ranking runs into a precision ceiling near 0.25, and the deployed majority vote (below) runs roughly 0.2 precision. In plain terms, when it does call an undercast it's right maybe one time in five, and it catches only a minority of real undercast days.

What the row-leakage bug was hiding (click to expand)

An earlier writeup here implied ~0.8 precision. That number came from the row-leakage bug mentioned above: folds were split by row rather than by date, so when a given forecast-hour row was scored, the other ~22 rows from the same day were sitting in the training set. The model didn't have to forecast an undercast — it only had to recognize a day it had already memorized.

Below is the same all-parameters model evaluated both ways, each panel at its own F1-maximizing threshold. The top row is the leaky split, the bottom row is the honest date-grouped split. Reproducing the old split here looks even better than the original writeup claimed — 0.9+ precision with nearly all undercast days caught — while the same model, same data, same algorithms scored honestly lands near 0.2 precision and misses most events. That collapse is the whole reason the deployed thresholds now aim for ~0.2: the old numbers were never forecast skill, just memorized days.

Two results worth pulling out. First, skill barely decays with lead — 0.947 at one hour against 0.877 at two days. The models are not merely recognising an inversion that has already formed; they genuinely forecast it. Second, combining all six sources beats the best single one by a wide margin (precision 0.45 against HRRR’s 0.19) despite training on a quarter as much data, because the models are wrong in different ways and the disagreement is itself informative.

Precision around 0.45 is still the weak spot: roughly half of the alerts are wrong. Some of that is the ceiling built into the training labels — the screen that generates them is itself only ~70% precise, so about three in ten training positives are not undercast.

Why two rows say “provisional”. While writing this up I found that every GFS value in the dataset had been sampled at 44.25°N, 0°E — in southwestern France, about 5,400 km from the mountain. GFS publishes longitude on a 0–360 grid, and asking for −71.30 does not raise an error; it quietly snaps to the nearest value in range, which is 0. The bug is fixed and GFS is being re-downloaded now. Until that finishes, the GFS row is blank rather than wrong, and the combined row is marked provisional because it was trained with those columns present. Removing them outright slightly improves the combined model, so its numbers should hold up or get better.

Earlier results (first pass)

Everything from here down describes the first version of the model — trained on the 24 hand-labeled undercast days, with forecast fields sampled at a fixed time of day rather than at each observation’s own valid time. It is kept for comparison until the figures are regenerated.

No single model is used on its own. The deployed prediction is a majority vote — a day is called undercast only when at least two of the three algorithms agree at their chosen thresholds. Requiring agreement trims false alarms at the cost of some recall, which is the right trade for a page you'd check before driving to a trailhead.

Next Steps

The original version of this section argued that the binding constraint was data quantity — 24 undercast days from a single image per day is not much to learn from — and that the highest-leverage next step was more labels, not a fancier algorithm. That turned out to be right, and reading the summit observer’s remarks instead of hand-scoring photographs supplied roughly 286× more positive examples. Discrimination improved a lot: the combined model now separates undercast from not at ROC-AUC 0.95 an hour ahead and 0.88 two days ahead.

What did not improve as much is precision, which sits near 0.45 — about half the alerts are still wrong. The constraint has moved rather than gone away. It is now the labels themselves: the screen that reads the remarks is only about 70% precise, so roughly three in ten training positives are not really undercast, and no classifier can be cleanly better than the thing defining its target. The useful next steps are consequently about truth, not architecture — more hand-scored days to enlarge the honest test set, several images per day to pin down timing, and observed vertical temperature profiles from the Auto Road, which would measure the inversion directly instead of inferring it from a model that thinks this mountain is 700 m shorter than it is.

So it is better than it was, and still not something to plan a sunrise around. If you want to know whether it’s undercast, you’ll have to hike up yourself.
